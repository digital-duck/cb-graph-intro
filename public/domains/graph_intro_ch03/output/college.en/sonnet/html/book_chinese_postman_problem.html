Concept Book: Chinese Postman Problem

Graph Intro Ch03 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

Every concept in this book has been a stepping stone: graph traversal taught you how to move through a network; Euler paths told you when a route could cover every edge exactly once; minimum spanning trees showed you how to build cheaply. The Chinese Postman Problem is where those ideas converge into something you can hand to a city planner, a logistics engineer, or a public-health inspector and say, here is the optimal answer.

The problem asks: given a weighted graph representing a road network, find the minimum-cost closed route that traverses every edge at least once and returns to the start. Real postal carriers, street-sweeping trucks, snow plows, and utility inspectors all need exactly this guarantee — every street covered, no unnecessary backtracking, total distance minimized. The solution algorithm is elegant because it reduces a hard-looking routing problem to two classical results you already know. First, an Eulerian circuit exists if and only if every vertex has even degree. Second, if some vertices have odd degree, you find all pairs of odd-degree vertices and add the minimum-weight set of extra edges — a minimum-weight perfect matching — to make every degree even. Once the graph is Eulerian, a standard circuit algorithm gives you the optimal route. The total cost is the sum of all edge weights plus the weight of those repeated edges, and the matching step guarantees that repetition is as small as possible.

Because the hard work is just Euler circuits plus matching, the Chinese Postman Problem scales to surprisingly large networks. A city with tens of thousands of road segments can be solved in polynomial time, which is why it has displaced ad hoc scheduling in municipal operations from São Paulo to Seoul.

The same structure appears far beyond streets. Genome assemblers treat DNA fragment overlaps as edges and need every overlap covered to reconstruct a sequence. Network security auditors model a system's connections as a graph and must probe every link. Industrial robot arms follow weld seams or inspection paths that map directly onto edge-covering tours.

Pick one of those domains and ask: what does a graph edge represent here, what is its weight, and what does it cost operationally to repeat an edge? Answering those three questions translates the abstraction into a deployable tool — and that translation is the skill this book has been building toward all along. Choose the application closest to work you care about and build the model from scratch.
