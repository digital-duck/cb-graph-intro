Concept Book: Traveling Salesman Problem

Payoff

The Traveling Salesman Problem asks a deceptively simple question: given a set of cities and the distances between every pair, what is the shortest possible route that visits each city exactly once and returns to the starting point? Despite fitting in a single sentence, TSP is one of the most studied problems in all of computer science — not because route planning is exotic, but because the structure it exposes is everywhere.

The brutal fact that makes TSP matter is that no known algorithm solves it efficiently for large inputs. With $n$ cities, the number of distinct tours is $(n-1)!/2$, which grows faster than any polynomial. For $n = 20$, that is roughly $60$ trillion routes. This places TSP firmly in the class of NP-hard problems, meaning any exact solution requires time that scales exponentially with input size — unless a polynomial-time algorithm exists, which remains unproven. What the study of TSP delivers, then, is not just a solution method but a whole engineering philosophy: when exact answers are computationally out of reach, find approximations with provable guarantees, apply heuristics with measurable performance, and recognize when a problem is hiding a TSP structure so you can bring the right toolkit to bear.

Consider a concrete instance. A delivery driver must visit five addresses — call them A through E — and return to the depot. Listing every tour and computing total distance is feasible here: there are only 12 distinct routes. A student can verify by hand that the nearest-neighbor heuristic (always drive to the closest unvisited stop next) produces a tour within a reasonable margin of the true optimum, while the Christofides algorithm guarantees a solution no worse than $1.5\times$ the optimum. Working through even this five-city toy example builds the intuition needed to reason about approximation ratios, greedy traps, and the difference between a fast answer and a good answer.

That tension — tractability versus optimality — is what makes TSP the natural capstone of this book. Every major concept introduced earlier converges here: graph representations, complexity classes, greedy and dynamic-programming strategies, and probabilistic reasoning about solution quality. Mastering TSP means you can look at a logistics network, a circuit-board drilling schedule, a genome-sequencing assembly problem, or a telescope observation queue and immediately ask the right questions about structure, scale, and acceptable approximation.

The most rewarding next step is to pick one application domain and go deep. If supply-chain logistics interests you, model a real last-mile delivery problem, implement the nearest-neighbor heuristic, then benchmark it against a branch-and-bound exact solver on small instances — and watch where and why the heuristic breaks down.
