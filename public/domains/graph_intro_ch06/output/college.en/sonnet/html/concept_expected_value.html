Expected Value

The expected value of a random variable is the long-run average outcome you would observe if you repeated an experiment many times. It does not predict any single trial — it describes where outcomes cluster on average. Formally, for a discrete random variable $X$ taking values $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n$ with probabilities $p_1, p_2, \ldots, p_n$:

$$E[X] = \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i \cdot p_i$$

This is a weighted average: each outcome is weighted by how likely it is to occur.

Worked example. Suppose a carnival game costs $\$2$ to play. You roll a standard six-sided die: rolling a 6 pays $\$8$, rolling a 4 or 5 pays $\$3$, and anything else pays nothing. What is your expected net gain per play?

First, compute expected gross winnings:

$$E[\text{winnings}] = 8 \cdot \frac{1}{6} + 3 \cdot \frac{2}{6} + 0 \cdot \frac{3}{6} = \frac{8}{6} + \frac{6}{6} = \frac{14}{6} \approx \$2.33$$

Subtract the $\$2$ entry fee:

$$E[\text{net}] = 2.33 - 2.00 = +\$0.33$$

On average, you gain about 33 cents per play — the game favors you slightly. A carnival operator would lose money long-term at these odds, which is why real carnival games are designed so $E[\text{net}] < 0$ for the player.

Problem-solving application. Expected value drives decisions wherever outcomes are uncertain. An insurance company pricing a policy calculates the expected payout across thousands of customers and sets premiums above that figure to ensure profit. A software team deciding whether to refactor a legacy module might estimate: a 30% chance the refactor saves 40 hours of future debugging, a 50% chance it saves 10 hours, and a 20% chance it introduces new bugs costing 15 hours. The expected savings are:

$$E[\text{hours saved}] = 0.30(40) + 0.50(10) + 0.20(-15) = 12 + 5 - 3 = 14 \text{ hours}$$

If the refactor itself costs 10 hours, the expected net benefit is positive — a rational argument for doing it. The key skill is identifying all outcomes, assigning realistic probabilities, and computing the weighted sum. Expected value does not guarantee a specific result; it tells you which choice is better in expectation across repeated or analogous decisions.
