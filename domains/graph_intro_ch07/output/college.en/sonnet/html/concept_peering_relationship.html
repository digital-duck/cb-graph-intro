Peering Relationship

A peering relationship is a mutual agreement between two networks to exchange traffic directly with each other, at no cost to either party, rather than routing that traffic through a third-party intermediary. The internet is not a single monolithic network — it is a collection of independently managed networks called autonomous systems (AS), each identified by a unique AS number. When two autonomous systems peer, they connect their infrastructure at a shared exchange point and agree to carry each other's traffic to their respective customers. Neither party pays the other; the benefit is symmetric.

This stands in contrast to a transit relationship, where a smaller network pays a larger upstream provider to carry its traffic anywhere on the internet. Peering is typically reserved for networks of roughly equal size and traffic volume, because both sides need to receive comparable value from the arrangement.

Worked example. Suppose University Network A (AS 1001) and University Network B (AS 1002) are both connected to a regional Internet Exchange Point (IXP). Without peering, a student at A downloading data from B must route traffic through A's commercial ISP, across the backbone, and down through B's commercial ISP — incurring latency and transit fees at every hop. After establishing a peering agreement, A and B connect directly at the IXP. Traffic between their campuses travels one hop instead of five, latency drops from ~40 ms to ~4 ms, and neither university pays transit fees for that traffic volume.

Problem-solving application. A network engineer at a mid-size content delivery company notices that 30% of their outbound traffic is destined for customers of a single large broadband ISP. Routing this through their upstream transit provider costs \$8 per Mbps per month. They estimate the equivalent peering session would cost roughly \$2,000/month in IXP port fees but eliminate \$15,000/month in transit charges for that traffic segment. The break-even calculation is straightforward:

$$\text{Monthly savings} = \text{Transit cost eliminated} - \text{IXP port fee} = \$15{,}000 - \$2{,}000 = \$13{,}000$$

The engineer then checks whether the ISP meets the company's peering policy — minimum traffic thresholds, geographic presence at shared IXPs, and operational contact requirements — before submitting a peering request. If the ISP qualifies, the two networks exchange BGP route advertisements at the IXP, and traffic shifts automatically to the direct path.

Peering relationships are governed by policy and economics, not protocol mechanics. BGP (Border Gateway Protocol) is the tool that implements the agreement, but the decision of whom to peer with is a business and operational judgment call made before a single packet is exchanged.
