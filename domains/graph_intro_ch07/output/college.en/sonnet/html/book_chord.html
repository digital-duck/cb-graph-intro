Concept Book: Chord

Payoff

A chord is a set of three or more pitches sounded simultaneously, chosen so that their frequency relationships produce a unified, recognizable harmonic color. Where a single note carries only pitch and timbre, and an interval carries only the tension between two pitches, a chord carries function: it tells a listener where they are in a key, whether the music is moving toward resolution or pulling away from it, and what emotional register the composer is inhabiting. That functional weight is why the chord is the natural capstone of a study that begins with acoustics and works upward through scales and intervals — every earlier concept feeds into it, and the chord is where those threads converge into something a musician can actually use.

To make this concrete, consider building a G major chord on a piano. The root G vibrates at approximately 392 Hz. The major third above it, B, sits at a frequency ratio of $\frac{5}{4}$ relative to G, landing near 490 Hz. The perfect fifth, D, sits at $\frac{3}{2}$, near 588 Hz. The three overtone series reinforce one another at shared harmonics, which is precisely why the major triad sounds stable rather than clashing. Change B to B$\flat$ and the ratio becomes $\frac{6}{5}$; the chord shifts to G minor, and the shared harmonics thin out just enough to introduce a quality listeners universally describe as darker or more tense. The same arithmetic you used to understand intervals now predicts the emotional character of chords without any further machinery.

From this foundation, an enormous range of applied work becomes accessible. Composers use chord progressions — sequences of chords whose roots move in predictable interval patterns — to create narrative arc in a piece, guiding a listener from tension toward release. Audio engineers and music-information-retrieval systems use chord detection algorithms that analyze the frequency spectrum of a recording and match energy peaks to known chord templates, enabling automatic transcription, key detection, and recommendation engines. Instrument builders must understand how a chord's combined vibrations interact with a resonating body — a guitar top, a piano soundboard — so that no single frequency overwhelms the blend. Even music therapy research quantifies emotional response to chord quality as an independent variable, separate from melody or rhythm.

Each of these paths rewards deeper study. If you want to follow one thread to its end, start with chord progressions and functional harmony: take any song you know, chart its chord symbols measure by measure, and ask why each change feels inevitable or surprising. That single question will carry you from Roman numeral analysis into voice leading, modal harmony, and eventually the full architecture of tonal music.
