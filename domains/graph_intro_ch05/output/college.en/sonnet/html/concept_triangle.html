Triangle

A triangle is a polygon with exactly three vertices, three edges, and three interior angles. The angles of any triangle always sum to $180°$, a fact that follows from the parallel postulate of Euclidean geometry and underpins nearly every calculation involving triangles.

Triangles are classified by their sides — equilateral (all sides equal), isosceles (two sides equal), or scalene (no sides equal) — and by their angles — acute (all angles less than $90°$), right (one angle exactly $90°$), or obtuse (one angle greater than $90°$). The right triangle is especially important because it connects the three sides through the Pythagorean theorem: if $a$ and $b$ are the legs and $c$ is the hypotenuse, then

$$a^2 + b^2 = c^2.$$

Worked example. A ramp rises $1.2\ \text{m}$ vertically over a horizontal run of $3.5\ \text{m}$. What is the length of the ramp surface?

Model the situation as a right triangle: the vertical rise $a = 1.2$, the horizontal run $b = 3.5$, and the ramp itself is the hypotenuse $c$.

$$c = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2} = \sqrt{(1.2)^2 + (3.5)^2} = \sqrt{1.44 + 12.25} = \sqrt{13.69} \approx 3.70\ \text{m}.$$

The ramp surface is about $3.70\ \text{m}$ long.

Problem-solving application. Triangles arise whenever you need to decompose a geometric situation into measurable relationships. For non-right triangles, two additional tools extend coverage to any triangle: the Law of Sines,

$$\frac{a}{\sin A} = \frac{b}{\sin B} = \frac{c}{\sin C},$$

and the Law of Cosines,

$$c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos C.$$

Use the Law of Sines when you know two angles and one side (AAS or ASA). Use the Law of Cosines when you know two sides and the included angle (SAS) or all three sides (SSS). For example, if a surveyor measures two sides of a triangular plot as $80\ \text{m}$ and $60\ \text{m}$ with an included angle of $110°$, the third side is

$$c = \sqrt{80^2 + 60^2 - 2(80)(60)\cos 110°} \approx \sqrt{6400 + 3600 + 3284} \approx 116\ \text{m}.$$

Triangles are the atomic unit of 2-D geometry: any polygon can be triangulated, making these three tools the foundation for area calculation, structural analysis, navigation, and computer graphics.
