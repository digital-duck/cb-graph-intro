Social Network

A social network is a model of relationships among a set of people, organizations, or other entities. It has two ingredients: nodes (the actors) and edges (the ties between them, such as friendship, following, collaboration, or messaging). Edges may be undirected (mutual friendship) or directed (A follows B), and may carry weights (how often two people interact). The key idea is that structure matters: who you are connected to, and how those connections are arranged, often explains behavior better than your individual attributes alone.

In code, a social network is simply a graph. The most common representation is an adjacency list, which stores each node's neighbors.

Worked example

Consider five students with these friendships: Ana–Ben, Ana–Cy, Ben–Cy, Cy–Dee, Dee–Eli.

friends = {
    "Ana": {"Ben", "Cy"},
    "Ben": {"Ana", "Cy"},
    "Cy":  {"Ana", "Ben", "Dee"},
    "Dee": {"Cy", "Eli"},
    "Eli": {"Dee"},
}
degree = {p: len(f) for p, f in friends.items()}

The degree of a node is its number of ties: Cy has degree 3, Ana and Ben have 2, Dee has 2, and Eli has 1. Cy is the most connected person. Notice also that Ana, Ben, and Cy are all mutually connected. This triangle is a tight cluster, which is typical of real social networks.

A useful measure of clustering is the clustering coefficient of a node: the fraction of pairs of its neighbors who are themselves connected,

$$C_i = \frac{\text{number of edges among neighbors of } i}{\binom{k_i}{2}},$$

where $k_i$ is the degree of node $i$. For Ana, $k=2$, and her two neighbors (Ben, Cy) are connected, so $C_{\text{Ana}} = 1/1 = 1$. For Cy, $k=3$ gives $\binom{3}{2}=3$ possible pairs; only Ana–Ben is connected, so $C_{\text{Cy}} = 1/3$.

Problem-solving application

Problem: Eli wants to be introduced to Ana. What is the shortest chain of introductions, and who is the critical person?

Use breadth-first search, which explores the network one "hop" at a time:

from collections import deque

def shortest_path(g, start, goal):
    queue, seen = deque([[start]]), {start}
    while queue:
        path = queue.popleft()
        if path[-1] == goal:
            return path
        for nb in g[path[-1]] - seen:
            seen.add(nb)
            queue.append(path + [nb])

print(shortest_path(friends, "Eli", "Ana"))
# ['Eli', 'Dee', 'Cy', 'Ana']

The path has length 3, so Eli is three hops from Ana. Every route between the left cluster and Eli passes through Cy and Dee. Removing either disconnects the network, so they are bridges or cut vertices. This is why analysts look for such nodes when studying how information, rumors, or diseases spread: a few well-placed individuals can control the flow between groups.

Large real networks show the "small-world" property: despite millions of nodes, typical shortest paths are only about six hops long, because a few long-range ties connect otherwise distant clusters.
