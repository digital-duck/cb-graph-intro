Concept Book: Affiliation Network

Graph Intro Ch08 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

Throughout this book we have built toward one structure: the affiliation network, also called a bipartite or two-mode network. It has two kinds of nodes, such as people and groups, authors and papers, or students and courses. Edges only connect nodes of different kinds. Everything earlier in the book (graphs, adjacency matrices, degree, projections) was a step toward treating this structure as a single object that you can analyze.

What it achieves

An affiliation network records membership directly, without flattening it. Let $B$ be the biadjacency matrix, where $B_{ia}=1$ if actor $i$ belongs to group $a$. Two projections follow from it:

$$ P_{\text{actors}} = B B^{\top}, \qquad P_{\text{groups}} = B^{\top} B . $$

Entry $(P_{\text{actors}})_{ij}$ counts the groups that actors $i$ and $j$ share. Entry $(P_{\text{groups}})_{ab}$ counts the members that groups $a$ and $b$ share. One data set therefore gives you two networks, and the diagonal entries give each node's degree.

This is the natural endpoint because it joins the earlier ideas. Degree, paths, clustering, and matrix products all appear in it. It also applies to many real data sets, because most of them record who took part in what, not who is directly connected to whom.

Worked example

Take three students and two clubs. Robotics has Ana and Ben. Chess has Ben and Cy. Then

$$ B=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad BB^{\top}=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&0\\1&2&1\\0&1&1\end{pmatrix}. $$

Ben's diagonal entry is 2 because he belongs to two clubs. Ana and Cy have an entry of 0 because they share no club. Ben is the only link between them, so he is a broker. Removing him disconnects the projection. Notice that the projection alone cannot tell you whether Ana and Ben share one club or three. The original bipartite structure keeps that information.

Application domains

The applications list for this concept was empty, so these are the standard domains where the structure is used:

  • Social networks: Co-membership in clubs, boards, or events reveals communities and brokers. Interlocking corporate boards are a classic case.
  • Recommendation systems: Users and items form a bipartite graph. Items that share many users, as in $B^{\top}B$, are candidates to recommend together.
  • Scientific collaboration: Authors and papers give co-authorship networks. The projection shows who works with whom, and its degrees show how productive each author is.
  • Biology: Genes and diseases, or species and habitats, form two-mode networks. Shared neighbors suggest functional or ecological similarity.

    • In each domain the method is the same. Build $B$, project onto the side you care about, then apply the degree and path tools from earlier chapters.

    An invitation

    Choose one domain and try it with real data. A small co-authorship set, a movie-rating table, or your own school's club roster all work. Build $B$, compute both projections, and find the broker. Then ask what the projection hid. The answer will show you why the bipartite form is worth keeping.
