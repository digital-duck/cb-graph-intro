Concept Book: Tunable Clustering Scale Free Model

Graph Intro Ch06 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

Every network model introduced in this book was an approximation. Random graphs gave us tractable math but no hubs. Preferential-attachment models gave us hubs but networks that were nearly tree-like — far too sparse in triangles to resemble anything biological or social. The tunable clustering scale-free model closes that gap. By grafting a triad-formation step onto preferential attachment, it produces networks that simultaneously satisfy two empirically measured signatures of real complex systems: a power-law degree distribution $P(k) \sim k^{-\gamma}$ and a clustering coefficient $C$ that can be dialed to match observed data. These two parameters — $\gamma$ and $C$ — are independent knobs, and that independence is the payoff.

To see why independence matters, consider the Holme–Kim construction. Each new node attaches to an existing node $u$ with probability proportional to $u$'s degree (standard preferential attachment), then, with probability $p_t$, also connects to a random neighbor of $u$, closing a triangle. When $p_t = 0$ you recover the Barabási–Albert model; when $p_t = 1$ you maximize local clustering. The degree exponent stays near $\gamma \approx 3$ across the full range of $p_t$, so you can raise clustering without distorting the hub structure. Generating a network with $n = 10^4$ nodes, $m = 3$ edges per arrival, and $p_t = 0.8$ in Python takes four lines with NetworkX:

import networkx as nx
G = nx.powerlaw_cluster_graph(n=10_000, m=3, p=0.8, seed=42)
print(f"γ ≈ fitted from log-log slope; C = {nx.average_clustering(G):.3f}")

Running this you will observe $C \approx 0.4$–$0.5$, comparable to measured values in protein-interaction and friendship networks, while the degree sequence still follows a power law.

That combination unlocks problems the earlier models could not touch. Epidemic spreading on a network with realistic clustering slows disease propagation relative to a tree-like scale-free graph, because triangles trap contagion locally — an effect measurable only once $C$ is a free parameter. Robustness analysis of power grids and internet backbones requires knowing whether targeted hub removal cascades differently when those hubs sit inside dense local communities. Recommendation systems built on co-purchase graphs need a generative prior that reflects the cliquishness of niche taste communities alongside the dominance of blockbuster items.

The model is the natural endpoint because it is the first in this book that is falsifiable at two levels simultaneously: you can fit $\gamma$ from a degree histogram and $C$ from triangle counts, then test whether the same $(p_t, m)$ pair reproduces both. That discipline — matching structure to data rather than just producing plausible-looking graphs — is what separates network science from network art.

Pick the application that interests you most — epidemic thresholds, infrastructure resilience, or collaborative filtering — and carry the model one step further: instrument the simulation, vary $p_t$ systematically, and measure how your outcome of choice shifts. The tools are all in hand.
