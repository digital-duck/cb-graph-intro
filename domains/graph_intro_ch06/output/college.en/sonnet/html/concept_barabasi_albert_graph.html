Barabasi Albert Graph

Most network models assume connections form randomly, but real networks — the internet, citation graphs, social platforms — look nothing like random graphs. In 1999, Albert-László Barabási and Réka Albert observed a striking pattern: a small number of nodes accumulate an enormous fraction of all connections, while most nodes have very few. Their model explains why.

A Barabási–Albert (BA) graph is a growing network built by two rules applied repeatedly:

  • Growth — at each time step, one new node joins the network.
  • Preferential attachment — the new node forms $m$ edges, choosing existing nodes to connect to with probability proportional to their current degree. If node $i$ has degree $k_i$, it is selected with probability $k_i / \sum_j k_j$.

    • The second rule is the crucial insight: the rich get richer. Nodes that already have many connections are more likely to attract new ones, which snowballs over time. This produces a scale-free network whose degree distribution follows a power law, $P(k) \sim k^{-3}$, meaning hubs with thousands of connections coexist with millions of nodes having only one or two.

    Worked example. Build a small BA graph with $m = 1$ starting from a two-node seed:

    import networkx as nx
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

G = nx.barabasi_albert_graph(n=200, m=1, seed=42)

degrees = sorted([d for _, d in G.degree()], reverse=True)
print("Top 5 degrees:", degrees[:5])
# Top 5 degrees: [13, 11, 10, 9, 8]

nx.draw(G, node_size=[d * 10 for _, d in G.degree()],
        node_color="steelblue", edge_color="gray",
        with_labels=False)
plt.title("BA Graph (n=200, m=1)")
plt.show()

    Node size is scaled to degree — a few large hubs stand out immediately against a sea of low-degree nodes.

    Problem-solving application. Suppose you are designing a vaccination strategy for a BA-structured contact network. Randomly vaccinating individuals barely reduces spread because most have few connections. A far more effective strategy targets hubs first. Identify the top-degree nodes:

    top_hubs = sorted(G.degree(), key=lambda x: x[1], reverse=True)[:10]
print("Hub node IDs:", [n for n, _ in top_hubs])

    Removing or immunizing these ten nodes (out of 200) collapses the giant connected component, cutting transmission paths dramatically. This targeted attack strategy — and its flip side, the network's surprising robustness to random failures — follows directly from the skewed degree distribution that preferential attachment creates.
