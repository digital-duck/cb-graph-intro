Tunable Clustering Scale Free Model

Real-world networks — social graphs, citation networks, the web — share two empirical signatures: a power-law degree distribution (a few hubs, many low-degree nodes) and high local clustering (your friends tend to know each other). The Barabási–Albert (BA) model produces the power law but generates almost no clustering. The Tunable Clustering Scale-Free Model, introduced by Holme and Kim (2002), fixes that by adding one extra step to BA growth, controlled by a single probability parameter $p \in [0, 1]$ that lets you dial clustering up or down while preserving the scale-free structure.

How it works. The algorithm runs like BA: start with a small seed graph, then at each time step add a new node and attach it to $m$ existing nodes via preferential attachment — nodes with higher degree are chosen with probability proportional to their degree. The twist is a triad-formation (TF) step: immediately after each of the $m$ edges is created, with probability $p$ the algorithm attempts to add one more edge connecting the new node to a random neighbor of the just-connected node, forming a triangle. When $p = 0$ you recover the original BA model; when $p = 1$ the algorithm tries to close a triangle after every attachment, maximizing local clustering.

The degree distribution remains approximately power-law, $P(k) \sim k^{-3}$, because triad formation affects only local structure without changing the global preferential-attachment logic. The average clustering coefficient $C$ rises monotonically with $p$, roughly as $C \approx \frac{m-1}{8} \cdot \frac{p}{m + p}$, giving explicit control over how cliquish the network is.

Applying it. Suppose you are modeling a professional network where people both seek out influential contacts (hubs) and tend to introduce mutual connections (clustering). You can calibrate $p$ by measuring the empirical clustering coefficient $C_{\text{obs}}$ from your dataset, then tuning $p$ until the simulated network matches. In NetworkX:

import networkx as nx

G = nx.powerlaw_cluster_graph(n=1000, m=3, p=0.5, seed=42)
print(nx.average_clustering(G))        # compare to C_obs
print(sorted(nx.degree(G), key=lambda x: -x[1])[:5])  # top hubs

Adjust p upward if your empirical graph is more cliquish; lower it if sparser triangles are observed. This tunability makes the Holme-Kim model the go-to synthetic baseline whenever you need a realistic graph with both hub structure and local cohesion — for example, stress-testing a community-detection algorithm or seeding an epidemic simulation.
