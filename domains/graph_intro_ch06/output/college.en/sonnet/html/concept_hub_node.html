Hub Node

A hub node is a vertex in a network that has a significantly higher number of connections (degree) than most other nodes. In graph terms, if a node $v$ has degree $\deg(v)$ much larger than the average degree $\langle k \rangle$ of the graph, it qualifies as a hub. Hubs are the connective backbone of many real-world networks — the busiest airports in an air-travel network, the most-followed accounts on social media, or the most-cited papers in a citation graph.

The concept gains precision through degree distribution. In a random (Erdős–Rényi) graph, degrees cluster tightly around the mean, so extreme outliers are rare. In contrast, many real networks follow a power-law distribution:

$$P(k) \sim k^{-\gamma}, \quad 2 < \gamma < 3$$

This heavy tail means a small fraction of nodes accumulates an enormous share of all edges — those are the hubs. Networks with this property are called scale-free, and hubs are their defining feature.

Worked example. Consider a simplified airline route map with six cities: Atlanta (ATL), Denver (DEN), Dallas (DFW), and three regional airports (R1, R2, R3). ATL connects to DEN, DFW, R1, R2, and R3 — degree 5. Each regional airport connects only to ATL — degree 1. The degree sequence is {5, 2, 2, 1, 1, 1}. ATL is the hub: removing it isolates every regional airport instantly, whereas removing R1 affects only ATL's count by one. This asymmetry — hubs as single points of failure — is the practical consequence that engineers and policymakers care about most.

Problem-solving application. Suppose you are analyzing a supply-chain graph and want to find which warehouse nodes are hubs. A clean computational approach:

import networkx as nx

G = nx.read_edgelist("supply_chain.csv", delimiter=",")
avg_degree = sum(d for _, d in G.degree()) / G.number_of_nodes()
threshold = 2 * avg_degree          # common heuristic: 2× mean

hubs = [node for node, deg in G.degree() if deg > threshold]
print(f"Average degree: {avg_degree:.1f}")
print(f"Hub nodes ({len(hubs)}): {hubs}")

Once hubs are identified, you can model targeted disruptions (G.remove_nodes_from(hubs)) and measure how dramatically the network's largest connected component shrinks — a standard resilience audit. Because hubs carry disproportionate traffic, even protecting or redundantly connecting a handful of them can make the whole system dramatically more robust.
