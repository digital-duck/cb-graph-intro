Concept Book: Food Web Modeling

Graph Intro Ch06 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

Food web modeling is where ecology stops being a catalog of species and starts being a science of consequences. By representing who eats whom as a directed graph — nodes are species or functional groups, edges carry energy-transfer weights — you gain a single mathematical object that encodes the stability, resilience, and vulnerability of an entire ecosystem. Pull one node out of that graph, reweight an edge because a pollutant cuts a predator's reproductive rate, or add an invasive species, and the model propagates those changes through every connected pathway. That capacity to simulate perturbation before it happens in the real world is what makes food web modeling a genuine decision-support tool, not just a descriptive exercise.

Consider a concrete scenario. Suppose you are advising a fisheries manager on the Atlantic cod collapse. You build a weighted directed graph: phytoplankton → zooplankton → capelin → cod → seals → orcas, plus dozens of lateral connections. You represent the system's dynamics with a system of coupled differential equations, one per node, where each species' rate of change depends on the weighted in-flow from prey and out-flow to predators. Even a simplified three-species Lotka–Volterra extension,

$$\frac{dx_i}{dt} = x_i \left( r_i + \sum_j a_{ij} x_j \right)$$

lets you ask: what harvest rate $h$ applied to cod drives the system to a new stable equilibrium rather than a collapse? The model gives a threshold; the fishery manager has a number to act on.

That same modeling skeleton transfers immediately across domains. In epidemiology, a contact network is a food web with pathogens as the "predator" — the same centrality and percolation tools that identify keystone species identify superspreader nodes. In supply-chain analysis, firms and suppliers form a directed graph where the failure of a critical node (a semiconductor fab, a port) propagates exactly like the removal of a keystone predator. In conservation planning, minimum viable ecosystem design reduces to finding the smallest subgraph that preserves all load-bearing energy pathways.

The mathematics you have built — graph traversal, eigenvalue stability analysis, simulation of coupled ODEs — does not change when the domain changes. The species labels do.

If you want to go deeper, start with the conservation application: take a published empirical food web (the Serengeti web is freely available), implement the Jacobian stability test, and ask which single-species removal most destabilizes the system. The answer will surprise you, and the code will be fewer than fifty lines.
