Degree Distribution

In any network, every node has a degree — the number of edges connected to it. A social media user with 500 followers has degree 500; a router connected to three other routers has degree 3. The degree distribution of a network is the probability distribution $P(k)$, which gives the fraction of nodes that have exactly $k$ edges. It is one of the most informative single statistics you can extract from a graph, because it reveals the underlying architecture of how connections form.

To compute it, count how many nodes have each degree, then divide by the total number of nodes $N$:

$$P(k) = \frac{\text{number of nodes with degree } k}{N}$$

Worked example. Suppose a small network has 10 nodes with degrees: 1, 2, 2, 3, 3, 3, 4, 4, 5, 7. Count the occurrences:

| $k$ | Count | $P(k)$ | |-----|-------|--------| | 1 | 1 | 0.10 | | 2 | 2 | 0.20 | | 3 | 3 | 0.30 | | 4 | 2 | 0.20 | | 5 | 1 | 0.10 | | 7 | 1 | 0.10 |

The distribution sums to 1. The mean degree is $\langle k \rangle = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i k_i = \frac{1+2+2+3+3+3+4+4+5+7}{10} = 3.4$.

Why it matters for problem-solving. The shape of $P(k)$ distinguishes fundamentally different network types. A random (Erdős–Rényi) graph produces a bell-shaped, Poisson-like distribution centered near $\langle k \rangle$ — most nodes have roughly the same number of connections. Real-world networks like the internet, citation graphs, and follower graphs instead follow a power law: $P(k) \propto k^{-\gamma}$, which appears as a straight line on a log-log plot. These are called scale-free networks. A handful of nodes (hubs) accumulate enormous degree while the vast majority have very few connections.

This distinction has practical consequences. In a scale-free network, removing nodes at random rarely disrupts the network because most nodes are low-degree. But targeted removal of the top hubs (highest-degree nodes) can fragment the entire network rapidly — a fact exploited in both cybersecurity (attacking infrastructure) and epidemiology (vaccinating superspreaders). Computing and plotting the degree distribution is therefore the first diagnostic step when analyzing any real network.
